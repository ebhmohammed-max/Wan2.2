Long before the Iron Throne was hammered from a thousand surrendered blades, before Aegon Targaryen ever looked west across the Narrow Sea, and millennia before the name of Westeros mattered to the great powers of the earth, a peninsula of fire rose from the southern waters of Essos. Its name was Valyria, and its mastery of dragons would remake the history of the known world forever.

Yet the civilization that would one day topple empires and melt stone like candle wax did not begin in palaces of obsidian or halls of gold. It began in mist and pasture grass, among quiet clans of shepherds tending their flocks beneath fourteen smoking mountains. How did a scattered people without kings, legions, or cities ascend to become as gods among mortal men?

To understand that transformation, we must look back roughly five thousand years before Aegon's Conquest—though, as the maesters of the Citadel in Oldtown repeatedly caution, every date from so remote an antiquity rests upon fragile parchment, weathered runes, and disputed temple scrolls. Humanity across the wide continent of Essos was still emerging from the long, traumatized shadow of a catastrophe remembered in every tongue: the Long Night.

According to the surviving accounts gathered in *The World of Ice and Fire*, bitter darkness had once gripped the eastern continent for an entire generation. Along the vast watersheds of western Essos, legend claims the waters of the River Rhoyne froze as far south as the joining of the Selhoru, lifting only when a hero persuaded the quarreling children of Mother Rhoyne—such as the Crab King and the Old Man of the River—to join in a secret song that brought back the day.

Farther east, beyond the towering Bone Mountains and the grim fortress-passes of Hyrkoon, the annals of Yi Ti preserve an even darker myth. Their priests speak of a primordial Great Empire of the Dawn shattered by the Blood Betrayal, when the Bloodstone Emperor murdered his sister, the Amethyst Empress, plunging the earth into night until a champion wielding a blade of living fire—called Azor Ahai, Hyrkoon the Hero, Yin Tar, or Neferion—drove back the gloom.

Where myth ended and history began, no scholar can say with certainty. What is clear is that when warmth returned to the world, great peoples blossomed across Essos—while Valyria remained unknown. Across the northern grasslands roamed the Qaathi and early fisher-kings of the Silver Sea, while along the gentle silver expanse of the Mother Rhoyne, the early Rhoynar raised peaceful river-towns of music and craft, becoming perhaps the first mortals to smelt iron.

At the opposite edge of the known map, where the poison-black waters of the River Ash crept beneath perpetual twilight, stood Asshai-by-the-Shadow. Even five millennia before the Conquest, Asshai was already incomprehensibly ancient—a vast, childless necropolis of greasy black stone that seemed to drink the light of torches, its original builders forgotten even by the masked shadowbinders and aeromancers who walked its silent quays.

Yet in central Essos, neither the river-singers of the Rhoyne nor the mystics of Asshai commanded the true center of worldly might. That prize belonged to the Old Empire of Ghis. Rising along the sun-baked eastern coast of what sailors called the Gulf of Grief, Old Ghis erected colossal stepped pyramids of fired brick behind thick defensive walls, crowned by a towering bronze Harpy clutching a thunderbolt.

Under its legendary founder, Grazdan the Great, Ghis forged an engine of conquest unlike anything Essos had yet witnessed: the lockstep legions. Armed with tall shields, short swords, and three spears of graduated length, Ghiscari infantry marched to the beat of bronze drums with mechanical discipline. Victory fed an insatiable slave economy, and slavery built the brick monuments of an arrogant, seemingly eternal order.

When Ghiscari captains stood upon their western ramparts and gazed across the blue waters of the Gulf, they saw no rival worthy of fear. They saw only distant plumes of gray volcanic smoke hanging above a rugged peninsula—a quiet backwater of grass and sheep. They could not know that inside those smoking peaks slept a force that would turn Old Ghis to ash.

Before cataclysm tore it asunder thousands of years later, the Valyrian peninsula was a vast finger of stone and emerald pasture thrusting southward into the Summer Sea. To its north and west stretched the warm, fertile expanse that men would call the Lands of the Long Summer; to its east lay the Gulf of Grief; and across its spine towered a natural fortress of fire.

The ancients named that mountain chain the Fourteen Flames. Arranged like a jagged crescent of titan peaks guarding the neck and spine of the peninsula, these fourteen active volcanoes never truly slept. By day, columns of white steam and dark ash trailed across the azure southern sky; by night, their high calderas pulsed with a sullen crimson glow, painting the bellies of passing clouds the color of freshly spilled blood.

To a cautious mariner sailing offshore, such a landscape looked cursed by angry gods. Yet volcanic fury carries an ancient ecological gift. Every eruption across millennia had blanketed the lower valleys in mineral-rich ash, weathering under warm coastal rains into dark soil of extraordinary fertility. Below the barren black scree of the upper cones, emerald meadows, wild olive groves, figs, and steaming geothermal springs flourished in unbroken summer warmth.

Beneath that green pastoral skin lay an even greater treasure, waiting silently in darkness. The deep roots of the Fourteen Flames were veined with immense deposits of red copper, tin, iron, silver, and gleaming native gold, alongside glassy seams of black volcanic obsidian—the frozen fire later Westerosi men would call dragonglass. Yet those subterranean labyrinths were neither empty nor safe, for things older than humanity stirred within the superheated rock.

Surviving accounts preserved by the Faceless Men of Braavos and studied at the Citadel speak of firewyrms—pale, blind, wingless burrowing creatures akin to dragons that breathed scorching flame and bored effortlessly through solid stone and magma crust. Young wyrms grew no thicker than a child's arm, while ancient specimens reached monstrous lengths in the lightless depths. For ages, the surface-dwellers wisely left that deep earth undisturbed.

Those surface-dwellers were the early Valyrians. The surviving histories tell us nothing of great kings, bronze-armored emperors, or conquering warlords among them in this dawn era, because none existed. They were a small, peaceful, inward-looking society of sheepherders. In circular dry-stone homesteads of stacked basalt and cedar rafters, clan elders guided long-wooled flocks across the highland pastures, measuring wealth not in slaves or spears, but in healthy livestock and woven wool.

Even as humble herdsmen, however, the Valyrians stood physically apart from every neighboring people in Essos. Where the Ghiscari possessed warm olive-amber skin and coarse dark-and-copper hair, and the river-folk of the Rhoyne bore sun-browned complexions and dark eyes, the shepherds of the peninsula possessed pale alabaster skin, hair of luminous silver-gold or spun platinum white, and striking eyes of lilac, pale indigo, and deep amethyst violet.

Centuries later, awed outsiders would call such beauty unearthly, almost more than human. Yet why did a quiet community of isolated herdsmen bear features found almost nowhere else in the known world? Most maesters of the Citadel propose a grounded natural answer: ages of geographic isolation trapped between sea and volcano, reinforced by close kinship marriage among a small founding population, fixed rare recessive traits of silver hair and purple eyes across the clans.

Other commentators, more inclined toward arcane lore, have wondered aloud whether the early Valyrians descended from some older, forgotten Dawn Age lineage—pointing to curious echoes in the myths of the Far East, where legends spoke of an Amethyst Empress with eyes of violet, though no proof connects the two. The truth remains buried in pre-literate mist; no chronicle survives in the Valyrians' own earliest shepherd tongue.

For generations uncounted, these silver-haired shepherds kept an unspoken covenant with their mountains: graze the green foothills, honor the rumbling earth, and shun the sulfurous vents above the tree line. Yet seasons turn, flocks stray toward high mineral licks, and bold young herdsmen inevitably climb higher than their fathers dared—up into the black basalt throats of the Fourteen Flames, where sheep began vanishing without a trace.

Above six thousand feet on the slopes of the Fourteen Flames, the world of grass and olive trees died abruptly. In its place stretched a cathedral of shattered black glass, hexagonal basalt pillars, and steaming yellow sulfur fissures. Here, where the mountain air tasted of iron and ash, early Valyrian climbers found the grim answer to their vanished flocks: bones, blackened and half-melted into the rock.

No mountain wolf or shadowcat left stone vitrified into shining glass. Drawn by dread and wonder alike, the first witnesses crept to the thresholds of the great volcanic caverns opening beneath the caldera rims. There, coiled upon ledges warmed by rivers of subterranean magma, they beheld the true masters of the Fourteen Flames—dragons, wild, untamed, and older than any kingdom of men upon the earth.

They were not heraldic ornaments or creatures of courtly song. In the living flesh, a wild dragon of the Fourteen Flames was an apex terror of jet-black iron-rich bone, furnace heat, and armored scale. Resting upon two massive taloned hind legs and the calloused knuckles of two vast, bat-like forelimb wings, an elder beast spanned two hundred feet from claw to claw, exhaling smoke from sulfur-crusted nostrils.

Every detail of their anatomy belonged to fire. Even their skeletons shimmered jet-black from the high iron content saturating their bones, lighter and more supple than forged steel. Beneath overlapping scutes thicker than bronze shields beat hearts hot enough to hiss mountain dampness into steam, before unleashing torrents of orange-and-black flame fierce enough to reduce flesh to ash and turn basalt ledges molten in seconds.

For the pastoral clans below, discovery brought terror long before it promised dominion. A single hungry dragon descending through morning mist could scatter an entire valley's flock in heartbeats, leaving behind nothing save scorched wool, blackened stone, and smoking craters. Yet fear did not drive the Valyrians from their homeland. Instead, something rarer took hold among their boldest youths: patient, relentless observation.

Hidden behind ridges of volcanic glass, watchers studied the beasts across years and generations. They learned when the great flyers hunted fish and leviathans over the Summer Sea and when they slept gorged upon cavern shelves. They observed clutches of hard, scale-textured eggs nestled directly inside geothermal ash-beds, watching winged hatchlings no larger than hounds claw free of smoking shells and hiss at the mountain sky.

Still, a deeper question haunted later historians who looked back upon those caverns: how had dragons come to dwell inside the Fourteen Flames at all? When Valyria stood at the height of its pride, its dragonlords offered a simple, self-glorifying answer. Dragons, they proclaimed, were the literal children of the Fourteen Flames—born directly from the womb of the volcanoes, bestowed uniquely upon Valyria.

Elsewhere in Essos, storytellers spun stranger fables. In the merchant palaces of Qarth, traders long repeated an enchanting myth: that two moons once hung above the earth, until one wandered too close to the sun and cracked from the heat like a giant egg, spilling a thousand thousand dragons across the heavens after they had drunk the sun's own fire. Maesters of the Citadel understandably dismissed such poetry.

Yet the Citadel could not so easily dismiss harder evidence pulled from the soil of distant lands. As Maester Yandel records, fossilized and weathered dragon bones have been unearthed thousands of leagues from Valyria—from the bleak, icy wastes of Ib in the Shivering Sea to the steaming, plague-ridden jungles of Sothoryos in the far south, where smaller, fireless kin called wyverns still hunt today.

Those scattered skeletons suggest a sobering truth: dragons once ranged across much of the prehistoric world, dying out everywhere else over unrecorded ages until only the volcanic sanctuary of the Fourteen Flames—and perhaps the farthest Shadow Lands—preserved living broods. Yet finding a dragon in a cave was one matter. How did simple shepherds compel a creature of living fire to bow its horned head to man?

If you consult the dark annals of Asshai-by-the-Shadow on how humble shepherds became dragonriders, you will hear a tale that strips Valyria of its proudest boast. In those bleak, candle-lit libraries beside the black River Ash, ancient scrolls insist that the Valyrians did not discover the art of taming dragons on their own—because dragons had already been mastered ages earlier in the Shadow beyond Asshai.

According to this Asshai'i tradition, a people so ancient that surviving memory preserves neither their name nor their unmasked faces first bent dragons to mortal will in the eastern darkness. Legend claims these nameless masters journeyed westward to the smoking peaks of the Valyrian peninsula, brought tamed dragons or binding lore to the silver-haired herdsmen, taught them their arcane arts, and then vanished utterly from the history of men.

Did masked strangers from the Shadow truly place the keys of world empire into shepherd hands? At the Citadel of Oldtown, maesters view that eastern claim with pointed skepticism. If an older civilization in the Shadow commanded dragons first, Maester Yandel asks, why did they conquer no kingdoms and raise no world-spanning empire as Valyria later did? Why leave behind no monuments save silent riddles in Asshai?

Yet even skeptical scholars concede one uncomfortable truth: simple pastoralists do not wake one morning fluent in high sorcery. Whether sparked by forgotten eastern teachers, stumbled upon through desperate survival, or refined across centuries of lethal trial and error among the calderas, the Valyrians unlocked something unprecedented in human affairs—not mere animal taming, like breaking a courser, but dominion forged through fire, glyph, sound, and blood.

Surviving fragments of Valyrian lore agree upon the instruments of that mastery. First came the horns. Hollowed from the curved horns of giant dragons or wrought of black iron and banded in red gold, these monstrous dragonbinders were incised with Valyrian glyphs of fire, blood, and command. When sounded, they produced no ordinary musical note, but a terrifying, bone-shaking scream that seemed to burn the air itself.

Millennia later, a single surviving Valyrian dragonhorn pulled from smoking ruins would blister lips and char the lungs of the mortal man who blew it, proving that every note demanded a sacrifice of living breath and blood. Alongside such horns worked incantations of High Valyrian fire-magic—words of binding spoken into cavern heat by seekers who risked cremation for a single mispronounced syllable.

Beneath the spells and horns, however, darker whispers persisted concerning blood magic. Thousands of years after Valyria's rise, Septon Barth—Hand to King Jaehaerys the Conciliator and perhaps the keenest student of dragonkind Westeros ever produced—dared to write in his *Unnatural History* that Valyrian bloodmages had delved into forbidden secrets of flesh, mating or altering creatures of fire and tying dragons to human bloodlines.

Conservative maesters denounced Barth's speculations as lurid heresy, and later kings burned most copies of his treatise. We cannot know whether Valyrian sorcerers merely bound wild dragons to specific families through blood-oaths, or worked deeper chimerical horrors in the volcanic dark. What history confirms beyond dispute is the result: a bond in which one rider claimed one living dragon—and the dragon accepted no other living rider while its master breathed.

Imagine the morning upon the Fourteen Flames when that threshold was crossed at last. High on a windswept ledge of black basalt, a young Valyrian clad in heat-cured leather and bronze chains lays bare hands against copper-bronze scales hot enough to raise blisters on mortal skin. Beneath him, a sixty-foot predator folds its vast wings just enough for a human figure to climb between its dorsal spines.

With a single thrust of leathery wings, stone falls away beneath them. Upward through banks of sulfur smoke and blinding coastal sunlight rises the first dragonrider—no longer a shepherd looking fearfully at the peaks, but a master of the sky looking down upon four horizons at once. Below him, valleys that took three days to cross on foot pass beneath a shadow in minutes.

In that single ascent, every wall ever stacked of brick or timber across Essos became obsolete, though the kings and emperors of the world did not yet know it. And if you value deep, book-faithful journeys through the ancient history of Ice and Fire, consider subscribing so you can follow every era of this chronicle from Old Valyria onward. For on the peninsula below, society itself was about to fracture and reform.

Power so absolute could never remain shared equally among simple clans. Not every shepherd family upon the Valyrian peninsula possessed a glyph-banded horn, knew the binding spells, or survived the lethal ordeal of approaching a nesting dragon. Those bold or ruthless lineages that succeeded rose almost overnight above their neighbors—ceasing to be herdsmen of sheep, and becoming shepherds of men and fire.

From this divide emerged the ruling caste that would define Valyria for five millennia: the dragonlords. Tall, silver-haired, draped now in crimson wool, spun gold, and overlapping bronze-and-iron scale, they viewed their mastery over dragons not merely as a skill, but as a sacred inheritance carried within their very veins. They began calling themselves *the blood of the dragon*—and guarded that blood with fanatical jealousy.

If the ability to command dragons resided in lineage—as their bloodmages taught—then mingling their blood with outsiders, or even with lesser non-riding clans, risked diluting the spell that kept monsters obedient. Out of that cold logic grew Valyria's most infamous custom. Rejecting the taboos observed by nearly every other culture, dragonlord houses wed brother to sister, uncle to niece, and cousin to close cousin.

Before ceremonial braziers of volcanic flame, brother and sister joined hands beneath the chants of blood-mages, binding estates, hatcheries, and sorcerous affinity tightly inside a single house. Generation after generation, these closed marriages preserved the luminous platinum hair, deep amethyst eyes, and uncanny dragon-affinity of the high nobility—while nurturing an unshakable conviction that dragonlords stood closer to gods than to ordinary men.

Yet this new aristocracy faced an immediate political paradox. Across ancient Essos, every great civilization bowed to a supreme monarch—the emperors and Grazdans of Old Ghis, the god-emperors of Yi Ti, the tall chariot-kings of Sarnor. Why, then, did no single conqueror among the Valyrians seize a crown and proclaim himself King of the Fourteen Flames? The answer flew in the skies above them.

A mortal king can compel obedience from subjects armed with bronze spears. It is far harder to crown oneself master over thirty-nine rival families when every rival household also commands living beasts capable of burning palaces to molten slag in a single afternoon. Any house that reached too openly for a throne risked uniting every other dragonlord lineage against it in a war of mutual annihilation.

Out of mutual deterrence and ancient clan pride, the Valyrians devised a radically different state: the Valyrian Freehold. By founding custom and constitutional law, Valyria acknowledged neither king nor emperor upon its soil. Instead, every free citizen who owned land upon the peninsula—every *freeholder*—nominally possessed a voice in the governance of the commonwealth, gathering in great open assemblies beneath the smoking peaks.

In practice, however, not all freeholders spoke with equal thunder. High above the common landowners, shipwrights, and lesser gentry stood forty great dragonlord families whose wealth, sorcery, and winged mounts eclipsed the rest of society combined. Within the colonnaded council halls of rising Valyria, these forty houses contended ceaselessly for primacy through marriage pacts, factional votes, arcane secrets, and calculated displays of strength.

When crisis or civic administration demanded unified executive authority, the freeholders and great houses elected *archons* to govern for strictly limited terms, ensuring no magistrate could turn temporary office into hereditary monarchy. Centuries and millennia later, among those forty noble families would stand a house named Targaryen—though in the grand hierarchy of Valyria, even the Targaryens would rank far from the mightiest.

In its youth, this republic of rival demigods proved astonishingly dynamic. Fierce competition among forty proud houses spurred every family to seek larger dragons, deeper sorceries, richer estates, and grander monuments than its peers. Dry-stone sheepfolds and timber halls could no longer house the lords of the sky. Valyria demanded a capital worthy of immortals—and its builders refused to use chisels and mortar.

Everywhere else in the ancient world, great architecture meant the slow agony of iron chisels, wooden rollers, mud bricks, and lime mortar. Old Ghis stacked millions of baked bricks into stepped pyramids; river-masons along the Rhoyne dressed blocks of rose sandstone. The dragonlords and fire-mages of young Valyria looked upon raw volcanic rock and saw something entirely different: clay waiting for a hotter kiln.

Combining concentrated dragonflame with the secret arts of pyromancy, Valyrian sorcerers heated quarried stone until it softened and flowed like molten wax. Guided by spells and shaped while incandescent, the rock cooled into seamless, monolithic ramparts of fused black stone—harder than granite, impervious to chisel or storm, and showing neither mortar joint nor mason's seam anywhere upon its gleaming dark surface.

Across the basalt ridges straddling the Fourteen Flames, an unearthly skyline began to climb into the smoky heavens. Where mortal masons stacked square and heavy battlements, Valyrian sorcerers twisted fused black stone into sweeping, organic spires that soared hundreds of feet aloft, opening at their crowns into wind-swept sky-courts and heated roosts built not for human footsteps, but for descending dragons.

Below those rising towers, engineers channeled rivers of living magma through fused-stone viaducts and open canals that lit the young metropolis from beneath with an eternal amber-crimson glow. Along grand processional causeways, master shapers formed colossal Valyrian sphinxes—beasts with the crouched bodies of dragons and the serene, violet-eyed faces of men and women—guarding avenues of polished obsidian, red gold, and bronze.

From the heart of their growing capital, the Freehold thrust its first dragonroads outward across the peninsula toward the sea. Unlike rutted earthen tracks, a Valyrian highway was fused directly into the bedrock: a broad, unyielding ribbon of dark gray-black stone raised half a foot above the soil, wide enough for three wagons abreast, arrow-straight, and immune to weeds or frost for millennia.

In blazing volcanic foundries fed by geothermal vents, Valyrian smiths pushed metallurgy beyond the limits of the ancient Bronze Age. Working copper, tin, silver, and high-grade iron ores alongside prized jet-black dragonbone—valued across Essos for bow-staves stronger than yew—they laid the experimental foundations for the spell-forged blades that later ages would revere in awe as Valyrian steel. Yet every wonder above carried a price below.

Soaring spires, golden ornaments, sorcerous instruments, and forty competing noble households devoured mineral wealth on a staggering scale. The richest seams of gold, silver, red copper, and iron lay locked deep inside the blistering roots of the Fourteen Flames—in suffocating shafts where free Valyrian citizens increasingly refused to labor. Thus, at the very dawn of its glory, the Freehold embraced its darkest sin.

Through border raids, debt bondage, and brisk commerce with neighboring slave markets, captives from across the region were driven down into the subterranean furnace beneath the volcanoes. Stripped to rags in air thick with sulfur fumes and choking ash, chained miners hacked at glittering ore veins beside stone hot enough to blister bare palms, hoisting heavy baskets up yawning black chasms.

Death waited in every tunnel. Sudden jets of scalding red steam boiled men where they stood; poisonous volcanic gases snuffed out whole gangs without warning; and in the deepest shafts, where pickaxes broke through into ancient magma tubes, pale wingless firewyrms burst from the stone to incinerate intruders in the dark. Still the dragonlords demanded ever deeper shafts and ever more hands.

Here lay the tragic contradiction built into the bedrock of Old Valyria. Above the clouds rose a shining commonwealth that boasted it bowed to no king, where noble assemblies debated law amid sphinxes and hanging gardens. Beneath their sandals groaned an expanding underworld of chains—an appetite for enslaved labor that would soon bring young Valyria face-to-face with the greatest slave empire on earth.

Within a few transformative generations, the peninsula of the Fourteen Flames had become unrecognizable. Every valley from the northern foothills to the southern capes—looking out toward the green Isle of Cedars—answered to the Freehold. Along turquoise bays of the Summer Sea, sleek merchant hulls crowded beneath fused-stone breakwaters, trading Valyrian gold, silver, obsidian, and fine metalwork with mariners from distant coasts.

Sailors returning from Valyrian waters carried breathless reports into every harbor tavern and royal court of Essos. They spoke of seamless black towers grown without chisels from living flame, of silver-haired lords and ladies with eyes like bruised plums, and of winged leviathans circling volcanic peaks like hawks above a pasture. Across the narrow waters of the Gulf of Grief, one empire listened with cold displeasure.

In Old Ghis, the heirs of Grazdan the Great had ruled central Essos unchallenged for centuries upon centuries. From the shaded terraces of their stepped brick pyramids, patricians draped in gold-fringed *tokars* surveyed harbors packed with bronze-rammed war-galleys and slave transports. In Ghiscari eyes, civilization itself meant Ghiscari law, Ghiscari brick, Ghiscari chains, and the unbreakable spear-walls of the lockstep legions.

Now, scarcely across the gulf, an upstart republic of former sheepherders was unearthing mountains of gold and silver, bidding up the price of enslaved captives in coastal markets, and refusing homage to the Bronze Harpy. Worse still were the verified sightings of tamed dragons. To the warlords of Ghis, such creatures were simultaneously an intolerable danger and an irresistible prize that belonged to Ghis alone.

In the shadow of the Harpy's thunderbolt, Ghiscari commanders weighed their young neighbor and felt serene certainty of triumph. Dragons, they reasoned, were mere beasts of flesh, and beasts could be brought down by heavy scorpion bolts, blinded by volleys of bronze-tipped arrows, or starved of riders. What were forty squabbling houses without a king against tens of thousands of legionaries marching behind triple spears?

Across the water upon the Valyrian peninsula, the young Freehold watched eastern war-galleys multiply along the horizon and felt neither dread nor submission. The descendants of shepherds had looked into the fiery mouths of fourteen volcanoes and bound living flame to their own blood. Having refused to kneel to any king of Valyrian bone, they would never kneel to the brick pyramids of Old Ghis.

Pause here, upon the knife-edge before the storm, and consider how far this chronicle has already traveled—and how much of its dawn remains deliberately veiled in shadow. History still cannot prove whether dragons hatched solely from the Fourteen Flames or survived there from a wider prehistoric world, nor whether nameless masters from Asshai ever whispered the first binding spells into Valyrian ears.

What surviving records confirm is the marvel—and the terror—that stood when the mists parted: a quiet pastoral peninsula transformed into an engine of unearthly beauty, high sorcery, fierce republic, and subterranean bondage. Out of wool and volcanic ash had risen forty houses of silver and violet, seamless spires of fused black stone, mines wet with captive sweat, and skies ruled by living fire.

Two worlds now stared across a narrow sea of gathering grief—the oldest military empire of central Essos, armed with bronze, discipline, and pride, and the youngest superpower on earth, armed with fire and blood.
