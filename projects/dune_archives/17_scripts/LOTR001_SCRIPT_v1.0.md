## H: Hook

> On screen, he is an eye. A great eye of fire, burning on top of a black tower, watching everything.

> But the books tell a very different story.
> In the books, one of the wisest Elves in Middle-earth says something strange about him. "For nothing is evil in the beginning. Even Sauron was not so."
> Before the Dark Lord, before the Ring, before the Eye, he had another name. A name that meant admirable.

> So who was Sauron, before he was evil?

## Prologue: The Council of Elrond

> In Rivendell, in the autumn, a council gathers in the house of Elrond. Elves, Dwarves, Men, two hobbits, and a third hobbit hiding in the corner, uninvited.
> Frodo holds up a little ring of gold. Plain, smooth, and the most dangerous thing in the world.

> A man of Gondor asks: why not use it against the Enemy? And Elrond says no. Whoever wields it will only become the next Dark Lord.
> "For nothing is evil in the beginning," he says. "Even Sauron was not so."

> It is a single line, and the story moves on. But the books never forget it. They tell us exactly how a spirit of light became the Dark Lord.
> And it begins long before the Sun, in a forge.

## Chapter I: The Smith's Apprentice

> Before the world was made, there were spirits. The greatest were the Valar, the powers of the world. Beneath them served the Maiar: lesser spirits, but mightier than any king.
> One of the Valar was Aulë, the smith. He shaped the mountains and loved stone, and metal, and gems. He loved making things, and he loved to teach.

> Among the Maiar of his forge, one stood out. The books say he was mighty in the lore of Aulë's people, and remained so to the end.
> In notes published long after the books, we even learn his first name. Mairon. In the elvish tongue, it means the admirable.

> And he was. His gift was order. He loved planning, and structure, and things working as they were meant to. He disliked confusion, and waste.
> It was his virtue. And according to those same notes, it was also the cause of his fall.

## Chapter II: The Admirable and the Abhorred

> Among the Valar, one would not wait. Melkor, the mightiest of them all, wanted to shape the world alone, quickly, and his way.
> The others worked slowly. They debated, and waited, and let things grow. To a mind like Mairon's, it must have looked like chaos.

> Melkor looked like a master who got things done. That, the notes say, is what first drew Mairon to him. Not cruelty. Efficiency.
> In the beginning of the world, the books say, Melkor seduced him to his side. The smith's apprentice left the light and knelt before his new master in the dark.

> Melkor became Morgoth, the Black Foe of the World. And Mairon was given a new name by those who feared him. Sauron. The abhorred. The Grey Elves called him Gorthaur the Cruel.
> The books say he was less evil than his master in only one way. For a long time, he served another, and not himself.

## CA: Subscribe

> If you want to see Middle-earth the way it is in the books, subscribe to the Mythic Archives. Because the next part of this story takes place on an island of werewolves.

## Chapter III: The Isle of Werewolves

> In the wars of the First Age, the Elves held a white tower on an island in the river Sirion.
> Sauron took it. Fear went before him, and the defenders fled. The fair island became Tol-in-Gaurhoth, the Isle of Werewolves.

> The books now call him a sorcerer of dreadful power, master of shadows and of phantoms, misshaping what he touched, twisting what he ruled. Lord of werewolves.
> But his truest weapon was the lie. He lured an outlaw named Gorlim with a phantom of his wife's face at a lighted window, and promised to give her back if Gorlim betrayed his leader. Gorlim broke. Then Sauron told him she was already dead.

> One day, a band of Orcs passed the tower without reporting to him. Sauron had them seized. They were not Orcs. They were Finrod Felagund, the golden-haired Elf-king, and a mortal man named Beren, in disguise.
> Finrod and Sauron fought, not with swords, but with songs of power. Sauron sang of piercing, of opening, of treachery. Finrod sang of secrets kept, of the prison opening, and the chain that snaps.
> Sauron was stronger. And Felagund fell before the throne.

## Chapter IV: Lúthien and the Hound

> The prisoners were thrown into a pit. One by one, a wolf came for them in the dark, and not one betrayed the others.
> When at last it came for Beren, the Elf-king broke his chains and fought it with his bare hands. He saved his friend, and it cost him his life.

> Then, from the bridge, there came a song.
> Lúthien had come. Her robe was blue as the unclouded heaven, her eyes grey as the starlit evening, her hair dark as the shadows of twilight. Beside her walked Huan, the great hound of Valinor.

> Sauron sent his wolves to the bridge, one after another, and Huan slew each one in silence.
> So Sauron went himself, in the shape of a werewolf, the greatest that had yet been. Lúthien cast a fold of her dark cloak before his eyes. And Huan sprang.

> Wolf, serpent, his own true shape. Nothing Sauron became could break the hound's hold. At last he yielded, and gave Lúthien the mastery of the tower.
> He fled as a vampire, great as a dark cloud across the moon.

## Chapter V: The Door Back

> At the end of the First Age, the hosts of the Valar came in their wrath. Morgoth's fortress was broken, and Morgoth was thrust out of the world.
> And Sauron did something no one expects from the Dark Lord. He put on his fair form again, bowed before the herald of the Valar, and renounced all his evil deeds.

> The books add a remarkable line: some hold that this was not falsely done. That Sauron truly repented, if only out of fear.
> The herald told him to go into the West and face the judgement of the Valar. It might mean a long servitude, to prove his good faith.

> And Sauron could not bear it. Under Morgoth, his power had been great. He was ashamed, and he would not return in humiliation.
> So he hid himself in Middle-earth, and fell back into evil, for the bonds Morgoth had laid upon him were very strong.
> This is the hinge of his whole story. Not hatred. Pride.

## Chapter VI: The Lord of Gifts

> He told himself a story. The Valar had abandoned Middle-earth. Someone had to heal it, and order it. Why not him?
> In the Second Age, a stranger came to the Elves. He was fair, and wise, and generous, and he called himself Annatar, the Lord of Gifts.

> The High King, Gil-galad, would not let him in. But in Eregion, beside the walls of Moria, the Elven-smiths welcomed him. Their lord was Celebrimbor, grandson of Fëanor, and they hungered for knowledge.
> Annatar taught them. And with his teaching, they forged the Rings of Power.

> Then, in secret, in the Mountain of Fire, he made a ring of his own. One Ring, to rule all the others. Into it he poured a great part of his own power.
> But the moment he set it on his finger, the Elves were aware of him. They knew him, and saw that he meant to master them. In anger and fear, they took off their rings.

> Three rings Celebrimbor had made alone, without Annatar's touch. Those, Sauron never found. He took Celebrimbor and tormented him, and the lord of Eregion died without giving up the Three.
> On television, before Annatar, he wore the face of a man called Halbrand, a lost king of the Southlands. Halbrand is the show's invention. In the books, Eregion knew only one mask: the Lord of Gifts.

## Chapter VII: The Drowning of Númenor

> Then came the Men of Númenor, the greatest kingdom of Men that ever was. Their king, Ar-Pharazôn the Golden, landed with such a host that Sauron's armies fled.
> So Sauron humbled himself before the king, and let himself be carried across the sea. A prisoner.

> Within three years, he was the king's closest counsellor. He had the White Tree of the kings cut down, and raised a great temple to Melkor in its place.
> At last he persuaded the king to sail against the Undying Lands themselves, to seize the life that never ends.

> And the world was changed. The sea opened, and Númenor fell into the abyss.
> Sauron was in his temple when it went down. His body was destroyed, and his spirit fled back over the dark water to Mordor.

> But he had lost something forever. The books say he could never again take a form that seemed fair to Men.
> And the later notes add one last detail. Until the downfall of Númenor, he still called himself Mairon, the admirable. After the drowning, the admirable was gone.

## Chapter VIII: Four Fingers on the Black Hand

> He rebuilt. When the Last Alliance of Elves and Men besieged his tower, Sauron came out and fought Gil-galad and Elendil himself, and both of them fell. Then Isildur cut the Ring from his hand.
> Isildur wrote that it was hot as a burning coal, and that his hand was scorched.

> And here, the films and the books part ways. On screen, the Eye is the Dark Lord himself, a giant fire on top of his tower. In the books, the Lidless Eye is his emblem, painted red on his soldiers' shields, and the Eye rimmed with fire appears in visions.
> The books' Sauron has a body. In the Dead Marshes, Gollum, who was tormented in Mordor, whispers to Frodo, "He has only four on the Black Hand. But they are enough."
> Four fingers. Most readers believe the missing one is the finger Isildur cut away, three thousand years before.

> And when the Ring finally falls into the fire, a huge shape of shadow rises over Mordor, crowned with lightning, and stretches out a vast, threatening hand. Then a great wind takes it, and it is all blown away.

## CB: Outro

> So, who was Sauron, before he was evil?
> He was Mairon, the admirable. A craftsman who loved order so much that he forgot who the order was for. He was offered one way back, and his pride closed the door.
> Nothing is evil in the beginning.

> Could he have been saved, or was he lost the first time he knelt in the dark? Tell us in the comments. Like this video if the books surprised you, and subscribe to the Mythic Archives for the next record from Middle-earth.
