**Visual rules** (looks entries are added to `WESTEROS_BOOK_LOOKS.md` before the stills):
- **Children of the forest (the book look, NOT the show's leafy look):**
  - Small as human children but not childlike. Slight, quick, graceful.
  - Nut-brown skin dappled with paler spots like a deer's. Large ears.
  - Big gold-green eyes slitted like a cat's.
  - **Hands with three fingers and a thumb, sharp black claws.**
  - Leaves, vines and flowers woven into the hair; cloaks of leaves, bark leg-bindings.
  - Weirwood bows, obsidian blades.
- **Greenseers:** rare children with mossy green or blood-red eyes.
- **Giants (book look):**
  - 10–14 ft, shaggy fur thicker below the waist, grey with age.
  - Head thrust forward, squashed face, square teeth, tiny eyes.
  - Arms longer than legs, splayed horny feet. They ride mammoths.
  - Not the show's tall bearded men.
- **First Men:** bronze swords, great leathern shields, horses; ringforts of grey stone.
- **Andals:** iron and steel, seven-pointed stars carved into their chests (shown as healed scars on armoured warriors, never the cutting).
- **Weirwoods:** bone-white bark, five-pointed blood-red leaves "like a thousand bloodstained hands", carved melancholy faces, eyes weeping red sap.
- **Maester Luwin:** small, grey, a chain of many metals at his throat.
- **Bran** (8–9, Tully auburn hair, blue eyes) appears from behind or small. No show likeness.
- **Bloodraven:** a pale corpse-like figure wound into weirwood roots, seen in shadow only.

## H: Hook

> In every castle of the North, there is a godswood. And at its heart stands a tree with bark as white as bone, leaves as red as blood, and a long, sad face carved into its trunk.
> The Starks pray before it. But no Stark ever carved it.

> Long before the first king, before the Wall, before a single man set foot in Westeros, someone else lived here.
> Small, and quick, and dappled like a deer, with eyes like a cat's. They sang to the trees.
> And when men came, they broke the world to stop them.

> So who was here before the First Men?

## Prologue: The Maester and the Boy

> In the first book, Bran Stark lies broken in Winterfell. He has fallen from a tower, and he will never walk again.
> To pass the long days, Maester Luwin gives him lessons. One afternoon, the lesson is about the children of the forest.

> "They were a people of the Dawn Age," the maester says, "the very first, before kings and kingdoms."
> No castles. No cities. No men at all. Only the children, in the lands we now call the Seven Kingdoms.

> Bran asks if they are still out there. The maester says they are gone.
> But the wildling woman Osha, a prisoner in the castle, says something else. She says they still live, beyond the Wall.

> One of them was lying.

## Chapter I: The Dawn Age

> The books describe them in careful detail. They were small, about the size of human children, but they were not childlike.
> Their skin was nut-brown, dappled with paler spots like a deer's. Their ears were large. Their eyes were gold and green, slitted like a cat's, made for seeing in the dark.
> And their hands had only three fingers and a thumb, with sharp black claws instead of nails.

> They had no cities, no metal, no writing. They lived in caves, in crannogs, and in hidden villages in the trees.
> Men and women hunted side by side, with bows of weirwood and blades of black volcanic glass. Obsidian. The glass that, thousands of years later, men would call dragonglass.

> Their gods were the trees themselves. They carved faces into the weirwoods, so the gods could watch over the forest.
> And a rare few were born with eyes as green as moss, or red as blood. The greenseers. Through the weirwoods, they could see far away, and long ago.

> They were not alone. The giants lived here too, ten to fourteen feet tall, shaggy with fur, riding mammoths.
> The two peoples did not always get along. The maesters record a giant's grave in the North, with obsidian arrowheads still lodged in its ribs.

> For thousands of years, this was the whole world. Then, from the east, came men.

## Chapter II: The Bridge of Land

> In those days, Westeros and the eastern continent were joined by a bridge of land, the Arm of Dorne.
> Across it came the First Men. The maesters cannot agree when. Some say twelve thousand years ago. Others say eight.

> They carried bronze swords and great leather shields. They rode horses, animals the children had never seen.
> And they brought their own gods.

> At first, the histories say, the children welcomed them. But the First Men cleared the forests for their fields. Then they began to fear the faces in the trees. They believed the children used them to spy.
> So they burned the weirwoods, and cut them down.

> And the children went to war.

## Chapter III: The Hammer of the Waters

> The war lasted for centuries. The children had weirwood bows and obsidian. The First Men had bronze, and numbers.
> In the riverlands, one legendary warrior earned the name Brandon of the Bloody Blade.

> The children were losing. So their greenseers turned to the darkest magic they had.
> Legend says they gathered and called down the hammer of the waters.

> The sea rose. The land shook. And the Arm of Dorne broke apart.
> Where the bridge had stood, there was only water, and a broken chain of islands that we now call the Stepstones.

> It was too late. The door was closed, but the First Men were already through.

> Legend says the greenseers tried once more, in the North, at the narrow neck of land between the swamps.
> At the ancient fortress of Moat Cailin, one ruined tower still bears their name: the Children's Tower, where they called upon their gods to bring the hammer down again.
> Some say that is why the Neck is drowned and full of bogs today.
> But the Neck held. And the war went on.

## Chapter IV: The Isle of Faces

> At last, both sides grew weary.
> In the middle of a great lake in the riverlands, called the Gods Eye, there is a small island.
> There, the greenseers and wood dancers of the children met the chiefs and heroes of the First Men, and made the Pact.

> The First Men would take the coasts, the high plains and bright meadows, the mountains and the bogs.
> The deep woods would belong to the children forever. And no more weirwoods would be put to the axe, anywhere in the realm.

> So that the gods could witness it, a face was carved into every tree on the island.
> From that day it was called the Isle of Faces. A secret order, the green men, was formed to guard it. In the books, some say they guard it still.

> What followed were four thousand years of friendship. The Age of Heroes.
> In time, the First Men put aside the gods they had brought with them, and worshipped the old gods of the forest.
> When the Long Night fell, men and children fought side by side. And according to the Night's Watch, the children once gave the brothers a hundred obsidian daggers every year.

## Chapter V: The Seven-Pointed Star

> Then, the books say, a new people crossed the narrow sea. The Andals.
> They had iron and steel. And they had a new faith, the Seven, with seven-pointed stars carved into their own chests.

> To the Andals, the faces in the trees were idols.
> They cut down the weirwoods, hunted the children who were left, and swept away the kingdoms of the First Men, one by one.

> In the riverlands, there is a hill called High Heart. Around its crown stand thirty-one white stumps, the remains of a sacred weirwood grove.
> The songs say an Andal king killed the children there and cut the grove down. The smallfolk say the hill is still haunted by their ghosts.

> But one kingdom never fell. At Moat Cailin, the Andals were broken against the ruins and the bogs.
> The North stayed a land of the First Men. That is why, thousands of years later, the Starks still pray before a heart tree, and not in a sept.

## Chapter VI: The Show and the Books

> On television, the children of the forest have skin like bark and leaves growing from their bodies.
> And in one scene, they bind a First Man to a weirwood, and drive a blade of dragonglass into his heart. He becomes the first White Walker.
> The children, the show says, created their own destroyer.

> None of that is in the books.
> In the books, the children are not plants. They are small, dappled, golden-eyed hunters with claws.
> And the books never say where the Others came from. Nothing in them blames the children.
> In the books, the children were the ones who stood beside men when the cold came.

## Chapter VII: The Last Singers

> Beyond the Wall, in the last published novel, Bran finally finds them.
> They save him from the dead in the snow, and carry him into a cave beneath a hollow hill.

> One of them is called Leaf. She tells him that "children" is only what men call them.
> The giants call them the little squirrel people. Their own name is older: those who sing the song of earth.
> "Before your Old Tongue was spoken," she says, "we had been singing our songs for ten thousand years."

> Deep in the cave, Bran meets the last greenseer: a pale, withered man, half swallowed by the roots of a weirwood, his one red eye glowing in the dark.
> Through the trees, Bran begins to see the past. In one vision, he watches the First Men offer blood to the heart tree of Winterfell. He tastes it in his mouth.

> Where are the rest of them? Leaf answers that the singers have gone into the earth, into the stones, and into the trees.
> "That was in the dawn of days, when our sun was rising," she says. "Now it sinks, and this is our long dwindling."
> The giants are almost gone too. The mammoths are down to a few hundred.
> "In the world that men have made," she says, "there is no room for them, or us."

> But the old weapons still work.
> On a hill beyond the Wall, the Night's Watch finds a buried bundle of dragonglass. Months later, a frightened young brother named Samwell Tarly drives a dragonglass dagger into one of the Others, and it melts away.
> The children's glass, waiting in the ground for eight thousand years.

## CB: Outro

> So, who was here before the First Men?
> A small, singing people who carved the gods into the trees, and broke the land itself to defend them.
> They lost their world. But they kept their bargain, and taught the newcomers to pray.
> Were the First Men invaders who stole a world, or did the Pact make them its guardians?
> Tell us what you think in the comments.
> Like this video if the books surprised you, and subscribe to the Mythic Archives. The archive is always open.
